## Intro

Most injuries on a skate trip aren't the sjukhus-level kind — they're road rash, a rolled ankle, a hard bail that leaves you sore for a week. But knowing the difference between "walk it off" and "get this checked" — and being prepared *before* you leave — makes the difference between a bad afternoon and a genuinely ruined trip. This guide covers both: what to sort out before you fly, and how to handle everything from scraped palms to an actual hospital visit.

## Before you travel

**Check whether your travel insurance actually covers skateboarding — many don't by default.** Skateboarding, especially ramp/park/vert riding, is commonly classified as a "hazardous" or "adventure" activity requiring specific declaration. World Nomads, a major travel insurer, lists *"Skateboarding (ramp, half pipe, skate park, street)"* as its own named category in their activity-coverage list — meaning it needs to be explicitly selected, not assumed to be included under a standard policy.
*Source: worldnomads.com — Cover for Activities, Sports and Experiences*

**If you're in the EU/EEA/UK/Switzerland, get an EHIC or GHIC card before you go — it's free.** It gives you access to state-provided healthcare at the same cost as a local resident during a temporary stay. Important limits: it does **not** replace travel insurance, doesn't cover private clinics, and doesn't cover repatriation home.
*Source: European Commission — European Health Insurance Card; NHSBSA*

**Know what your embassy can and can't do for you.** If you're seriously injured abroad, your embassy/consulate can help locate medical services and notify family — but they will **not** pay your medical bills. The US State Department states this directly: *"a consular officer can assist in locating appropriate medical services... However, payment of hospital and other expenses is the responsibility of the traveler."* This is a common misconception worth clearing up before you need it.
*Source: US Department of State (travel.state.gov / exchanges.state.gov)*

**Practical prep checklist:**
- Save your destination's emergency number (it's not always 911/112 — check before you go)
- Save your embassy/consulate contact info and your insurer's 24/7 emergency line
- Carry a basic first-aid kit: adhesive bandages, gauze, antiseptic wipes, tape, an instant cold pack
- Know your own allergies/blood type and carry them written down (useful if a language barrier or head injury affects your ability to communicate)

## Part 1: Minor injuries you can usually handle yourself

**Cuts and road rash.** According to the NHS, most cuts and grazes can be treated at home: stop any bleeding with direct pressure, clean the wound under running water, and cover it with a clean dressing. See a doctor if there's a risk of infection — for example if the wound was contaminated with dirt, or if something is stuck in it (don't try to remove embedded objects yourself).
*Source: NHS — Cuts and grazes*

**Sprains and rolled ankles/wrists — the RICE method, explained properly:**

RICE stands for **Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation** — the standard first-response approach for sprains, strains, and bruises, used in the first 24–72 hours after injury.

![Illustrated four-step RICE method diagram: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation, each with a short instruction](./images/rice-method.png)

- **Rest** — Stop the activity immediately and avoid putting weight on the injured area. This isn't about staying still for days; it means protecting the joint for the first 48 hours before gradually reintroducing movement. Continuing to skate on a rolled ankle risks turning a mild sprain into a much worse one.
- **Ice** — Apply an ice pack (or a bag of frozen vegetables wrapped in a towel — never ice directly on skin) for 15–20 minutes at a time, every 2–3 hours. This helps numb pain and limit swelling, especially in the first two days.
- **Compression** — Wrap the area with an elastic bandage, snug but not so tight it cuts off circulation (numbness or tingling means it's too tight). This helps control swelling.
- **Elevation** — Keep the injured limb raised above heart level when possible, which also helps reduce swelling.

*Source: NHS — Sprains and strains*

**One honest caveat worth including:** RICE has been the standard advice since 1978, but more recent sports medicine research has questioned parts of it — particularly whether ice actually speeds healing (it may just numb pain), and whether prolonged complete rest can delay recovery by causing stiffness. Current guidance leans toward *protecting* the injury rather than fully immobilizing it, with a gradual, pain-guided return to movement rather than waiting until it feels 100%. RICE is still a reasonable first response — just not a guarantee of faster healing on its own.
*Sources: Cleveland Clinic; Yale Medicine*

**When RICE isn't enough — see a doctor if:** pain or swelling is severe, you can't bear any weight at all, or things aren't improving after a few days of self-care. RICE can make an injury more comfortable, but it can't tell you whether it's a sprain or an actual fracture.

## Part 2: When it's not minor — red flags that mean seek care

**Head injuries / possible concussion.** Most head knocks are not emergencies, but seek medical care immediately if you notice:
- Loss of consciousness, even briefly
- Repeated vomiting
- Worsening headache not relieved by pain medication
- Confusion, slurred speech, or unequal pupil sizes
- Symptoms that appear or worsen in the 24–48 hours after the fall

*Sources: NHS-affiliated Northern Care Alliance head injury advice; HSE Ireland*

**Other clear signs to get checked rather than self-treat:**
- A wound that won't stop bleeding with direct pressure after several minutes
- Visible deformity, or inability to bear any weight — possible fracture
- A wound with something embedded in it
- Any doubt at all about a head injury — err toward getting checked

## Part 3: If you end up at a hospital abroad

**Know that many countries expect payment upfront, even in emergencies.** This varies hugely by country — some public systems (with EHIC/GHIC coverage in Europe) charge little or nothing at the point of care; others, including the US and many private facilities worldwide, expect payment or insurance verification before or shortly after treatment. This is exactly why the insurance step above matters — check what your policy actually requires (pay-then-claim vs. direct billing).

**What to bring/have ready:**
- Passport or ID
- Travel insurance policy number and 24/7 assistance line
- EHIC/GHIC if applicable
- A note of any allergies, medications, or existing conditions

**What your embassy will actually do:** help you find an English-speaking doctor or appropriate facility, contact family on your behalf if needed, and in serious cases help coordinate next steps — but again, not pay your bill.
*Source: US Department of State; UK FCDO consular assistance guidance*

## Checklist

- [ ] Confirm your travel insurance explicitly covers skateboarding — check the activity list, don't assume.
- [ ] If traveling in Europe: apply for a free EHIC/GHIC before you go.
- [ ] Save your destination's local emergency number, your embassy's number, and your insurer's assistance line.
- [ ] Pack a basic first-aid kit.
- [ ] Know the RICE method for sprains and basic cut/graze care.
- [ ] Know the concussion red flags — don't self-diagnose a head injury.
- [ ] Understand your embassy helps coordinate care, not pay for it.
